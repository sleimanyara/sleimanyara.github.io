abstract: |-
  Research on motivated reasoning suggests that partisans who discount unwelcome evidence
  also learn less from it. Yet persuasion experiments find opposing groups updating in
  parallel, and some studies report greater movement toward counter-attitudinal evidence.
  We reconcile these findings through a meta-reanalysis of 41 experiments (30,731
  respondents) that measure both evaluations of political information and subsequent
  beliefs. Contrary to stronger accounts of motivated reasoning, we find that partisan
  bias is strong in how people judge political information but much smaller in how far
  their beliefs move in response (0.54 vs. 0.13 SD in designs least prone to measurement
  bias). We also find no evidence that partisans learn more from counter-attitudinal
  information. Instead, we show that this apparent pattern largely reflects regression to
  the mean arising from how belief change is measured. The results suggest that partisan
  divisions over evidence may pose less of a barrier to learning than is often assumed.
