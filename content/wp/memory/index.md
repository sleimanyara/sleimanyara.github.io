abstract: |-
  Bilinguals represent a growing share of the electorate in many established
  democracies, yet we still know little about how language shapes the processing of
  political information. We ask whether the language in which citizens encounter
  political information shapes what they remember and whether it moderates the partisan
  biases governing recollection. Secondary-language processing may disrupt motivated
  reasoning by encouraging effortful evaluation, or deplete the resources needed to
  resist it. In a preregistered experiment, English-French bilinguals in Canada (N =
  765) were randomly assigned to read pro- and anti-immigration arguments in their
  primary or secondary language before completing a recognition task. We find that
  secondary-language processing more than halves the congeniality gap in inferential
  memory, driven by a reduction in belief-consistent flawed memories. The findings
  identify language of processing as a moderator of partisan memory and demonstrate that
  linguistic context can disrupt the belief-consistent inferences through which
  political information becomes distorted in memory.
