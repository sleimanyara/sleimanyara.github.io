abstract: |-
  Debates about algorithmic government have largely focused on transferring
  decision-making authority from public officials to machines. We ask instead what is
  lost when AI inference replaces citizens' voice, and when citizens are willing to
  delegate voice to AI themselves. We argue that political voice has value beyond the
  information it provides, but that citizens may be willing to forgo direct expression
  when participation is costly and their preferences can be reliably inferred. To test
  these arguments, we propose two preregistered survey experiments involving 4,000 US
  adults. Study 1 examines whether citizens evaluate government procedures less favourably
  when their preferences are inferred rather than directly expressed, varying whether
  public officials or AI process the information while holding accuracy and
  decision-making authority constant. We expect inference to reduce procedural acceptance
  by weakening citizens' sense of being heard, even when their preferences are accurately
  known. Study 2 is a behavioural experiment where respondents choose whether to express
  their preferences themselves or allow a predictive system to answer on their behalf. We
  vary the effort required for direct expression and the system's predictive accuracy to
  measure the amount of effort citizens are willing to expend to preserve their voice. We
  expect delegation to increase with participation costs and predictive accuracy, but to
  remain less common for political than non-political preferences. Together, the studies
  contribute a framework for examining the value of political voice in an age of AI, both
  when it is denied by institutions and when citizens can choose to relinquish it.
