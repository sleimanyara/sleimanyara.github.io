abstract: |-
  Social identities increasingly sort Americans into opposing partisan camps, yet many
  voters still occupy cross-cutting affiliations that pull them in competing political
  directions. Existing work shows that cross-pressures shape turnout and participation,
  but tells us less about how they affect the choices voters make when they do turn out.
  This article shifts the focus from participation to decision quality. I argue that
  group cross-pressures undermine voters' ability to choose the candidate who best
  reflects their interests. Conflicting signals fragment issue positions, weaken
  partisan heuristics, and compress perceived differences between candidates, making
  'correct' voting more difficult. Using ANES data from five presidential elections
  (2008-2024), I show that cross-pressures impose a substantial penalty on correct
  voting that is not accounted for by standard predictors such as political knowledge
  and interest. Because these pressures are rooted in durable social positions, they
  constitute a structural constraint on electoral choice in the two-party U.S. system.
  These findings identify cross-pressures as a distinct source of inequality in
  electoral decision-making and complicate normative accounts of social sorting,
  suggesting that the same alignment that intensifies partisan polarisation may also
  facilitate representational quality.
